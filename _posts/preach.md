"VI: The Preach"

<!--Act I: The Construction of the Absolute-->
As I traveled, I worked. My unified template, a sprawling spreadsheet of hundreds of columns, became my constant companion, my mobile laboratory. I was on a quest to solve a fundamental problem: how to represent a stock's true position within its value range with a single, objective number. To this question, I developed a single, unified metric—a scale from one to five—that could represent exactly where a stock stood within its historical value range. For any value-driven stock, a one meant it was time to buy; a five meant it was time to sell. Later, I developed an even more crucial metric: a way to measure the degree to which a stock was value-driven, its "value-purity," so to speak. And from this, a complete philosophical methodology emerged.

I had, I believed, found the first principles. A stock is a company. A company is a value-generating machine. Therefore, its true worth can only be measured by value metrics. Everything else is just noise, speculation, a market of emotions that causes price to deviate from value. Just like buying anything else, you should buy when it's cheapest and sell when it's most expensive. I was convinced I had cracked the code, that I now held the best answer in the world to the problem of equity evaluation.

I looked at the existing landscape of knowledge and saw only a wasteland. A famous, expensive value investing website had its own unified metric, but it was a crude, unverified average of P/E, P/B, and P/S ratios. It made the same lazy assumptions as the entire tradition: that the relationship between price and value is linear, that all stocks are value-driven. It was the work of a primary school student compared to my professorial thesis. How could no one else have thought of this? How could no one else have questioned it?

My lifelong pursuit of knowledge had also reached a dead end. I read *Value Investing: From Graham to Buffett and Beyond* and found its arguments shockingly flimsy. The ultimate justification for value investing, it seemed, was simply that its practitioners, the "Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville," had beaten the market. Was this the pinnacle of human financial reasoning? A method whose only philosophical foundation was "it works because they are smart"?

I felt a profound sense of disillusionment. I was swimming in an ocean of knowledge, but I was dying of thirst. The books all repeated the same famous studies, the same tired theories. They offered a thousand different theories, but no one offered a theory on how to validate a theory—a question whose importance, by the simplest of logical deductions, far surpasses the former. Humanity has epistemology, a branch of philosophy dedicated to explaining what knowledge even is, yet it remains stuck at the most fundamental level, endlessly debating foundationalism versus coherentism, without ever building a practical, foundational methodology for how knowledge should be produced. In this vast ocean, I felt a powerful sense of being lost.

And in that moment of supreme intellectual achievement and profound intellectual loneliness, I stood at the peak of my own, self-built mountain of reason. I had the answer. I had the truth. But as I looked down from the summit, I saw that the world below was shrouded in a thick, impenetrable fog. I could shout, but my voice would only echo back at me, unheard. I had climbed so high that I had left humanity behind.

<!--Act II: The Failure of Transmission-->
I continued to refine my system as I traveled. Fellow travelers I met on the road would sometimes ask about my financial freedom. But how could I explain? To tell them the truth would require me to first declare the entire edifice of human knowledge to be flawed, to claim that my system was to all others as a professor is to a primary school student. Who in their right mind would believe me? We didn't even share a common language. Some would express gratitude for a small piece of the puzzle, a non-linear value metric I had developed, not understanding that a single gear is meaningless without the engine that drives it. Others would ask what books I had read to gain such insight, and when I could offer almost none, they would look at me with suspicion. I couldn't explain that the greatest value lies not in consuming a flawed knowledge system, but in having the ability to step back and question it from first principles.

The only ones who seemed willing to listen were my "classmates" from that fraudulent investment course. I gained about seven paying subscribers, each paying a not-insignificant fee of nearly $2,500. But I soon discovered that they didn't understand that my system was on a fundamentally different level from all others; they just saw it as another "hot tip," another seminar to attend.

I had designed my system to be impossibly user-friendly. When a value-driven stock hit the bottom of its reasonable range, it would light up. That was the signal to buy. It was simple. The system was so transparent, so reliant on objective data, that it left no room for the seductive narratives that other gurus used. I thought the results would speak for themselves, that my system would spread like a virus, growing its own snowball of success.

I was wrong.

Even after paying the hefty subscription fee, they barely acted. They just watched. A stock would light up, climb from the bottom of its range to the top, and they would do nothing. I couldn't understand it. Why pay for a system you refuse to follow? I had made it as simple as possible. I was literally giving them the numbers. One subscriber did renew. He had made a six-figure profit by placing a risky long-call bet on a single one of my signals—a complete perversion of the system's logic, but a profitable one. But after that one renewal, he too went silent, lost to the noise and distractions of the world.

I thought I had achieved something unprecedented, that anyone who spoke the language of logic would have to concede to my arguments. But there was no one. The few who paid did so out of a shallow trust, a "why not give it a try" mentality. And in the end, even the simplest, most logical system was no match for their own inertia and fear.

I published a long essay on Medium, "The Universal Laws of Investment Success," laying out my entire groundbreaking philosophy—from the flawed nature of back-testing and the necessity of causality, to the very first principles of building an unassailable investment method. I had, I believed, revealed the groundlessness of all existing investment systems and, for the first time in history, shown how to build one with a true foundation.

The response was a deafening silence.
