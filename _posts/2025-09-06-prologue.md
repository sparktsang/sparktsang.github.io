It began with a silence.

A question would be asked — [a simple, almost childish question][childhood] such as, *"What do you like about her?"* — and the world would seem to pause.

My mind did not receive a question as a single object. It unfolded into a structure: possible interpretations, hidden premises, motives, alternative answers, consequences. In the half-second before I spoke, a small question could become a labyrinth.

*Do I challenge the premise? Do I examine the questioner's motive? Do I ask what "like" actually means? Or do I simply give the expected answer and move on?*

On the outside, I was a child staring into silence. On the inside, I was already mapping the maze.

For a long time, I understood this as a defect.

I grew up believing that silence meant inadequacy, that hesitation meant weakness, and that the world rewarded those who could move through its rules without stopping to question them. I learned to distrust my own instinct to take apart those rules.

The pattern followed me.

I could sleep through classes and still ace the examinations. I could [construct elaborate systems in virtual worlds while struggling with the simplest negotiations in the real one][insurance]. I could understand a problem from every conceivable angle and still fail to act on the obvious answer.

The world had simpler names for this. 

Failure. Misfit. Alien. 

A classmate once called me the *"Prince of Mars."* Perhaps the joke contained more truth than intended.

I often felt as though I had been given a map to a place that did not yet exist. The map was intricate, absorbing, and sometimes beautiful. It was also useless when everyone around me was simply trying to cross the street.

For years, I thought the problem was the map. I was wrong. The problem was that I did not yet know what the map was for.

These stories are an attempt to reconstruct that discovery.

They are not conventional literature. They are an excavation: a record of the assumptions, failures, obsessions, relationships, systems and questions that gradually shaped the way I see the world.

They are also an autopsy.

Not of a failed life, but of a particular way of thinking — a mind that repeatedly tried to reduce a chaotic reality to a structure it could understand, only to discover that every map generated another territory.

The world as it is was never simple. But neither was the world I imagined could exist.

Between the two lies the real subject of these pages: the attempt to build a bridge between reality and reason, between what exists and what ought to exist, between the systems we inherit and the systems we might still construct.

Over time, the map changed. It became less interested in escaping the world and more interested in understanding its architecture.

The questions became larger. Then they became more dangerous.

And eventually, they became useful.

This is not a story of a man who found all the answers. It is the story of a mind that could not escape the questions — and eventually learned to turn the questions into instruments.

The investigation begins now.
