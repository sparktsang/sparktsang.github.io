The author’s elegant, highly educated friend, when talking about people who cheat, repeatedly brings up the words “illness” and “mental instability”; the author’s research into female infidelity is accompanied by intense curiosity as well as condemnation.  
The author finds it astonishing that people promise to stay together for life without ever discussing sexual exclusivity, but then realizes she is no different herself.  
Books on non-monogamy, affairs, and related subjects have sprung up everywhere. *When Good People Have Affairs* implicitly suggests that people think those who cheat are not good people; Tristan Taormino’s *Opening Up* has become a long-selling title; all her divorced female friends and friends in their twenties are reading *The Ethical Slut*.  
Three forms of non-monogamous relationships: open relationships (you can fool around; I don’t want to know); swingers (the two of you play around with others, sometimes together, sometimes separately, but you may discuss it); polyamory (multiple partners, usually initiated by women).  
Therapist Mark Kaupp, who supports non-monogamous relationships, argues that the greater your attachment security, the more love you are capable of giving—even beyond one person, potentially to an unlimited number of people.  
The proportion of people who cheat at some point in their lives is 20–37.5% (Walker, 2001; S. Luo, 2010). It is difficult to obtain precise statistics because people are reluctant to admit it.  
In 2010, 40% of Americans said marriage was becoming obsolete. That year, 51% of Americans were married, the lowest figure in history.  
In 1976, 50% of highly educated GSS respondents said having an affair was always wrong; by 2008, the figure had risen to 75%. Yet people had become more tolerant than before of divorce, premarital sex, and homosexuality. Infidelity had become the final sexual taboo.  

According to the 2013 General Social Survey, women cheating on their husbands had increased by 40% compared with 1990, while the rate for men remained as before.  
After excluding outliers, the ratio between women and men was almost the same (P. Druckerman, 1993, 2008); four national surveys conducted between 1991 and 1996 showed that among people under 45, the rates of infidelity for women and men were nearly identical (D. C. Atkins, 2001).  
A 2017 study found that among women aged 25–29, experience with group sex and threesomes was the same as among men of the same age (D. Herbenick, 2017).  
Women experience opportunities for alternative forms of sex—including sex dungeons, leather/BDSM, polyamory, and sex parties—at nearly twice the rate of men.  
Women frequently experience responsive sexual desire, a concept associated with Rosemary Basson: they become aroused only after encountering sexual stimulation, rather than experiencing spontaneous desire (like feeling hungry and wanting to eat).  
A 2014 study found that men and women described the intensity of desire in essentially the same terms. Meredith Chivers of the Sexuality and Gender Laboratory argued that the way desire had traditionally been measured might be flawed; if responsive sexual desire were measured instead, the results should be the same.  
The following are all findings from Chivers’s research: heterosexual women’s bodies responded regardless of what kind of pornography they watched, including footage of bonobo mating; straight men, by contrast, showed their strongest responses to scenes that matched their self-reported sexual preferences.  
For a heterosexual woman, sex with a male friend produced less arousal than sex with either a long-term partner or a stranger.  
When imagining having sex with a stranger, female subjects showed levels of arousal comparable to those of straight men.  
A 2017 study of 11,000 British men and women aged 16 to 74 found that living with a partner was twice as likely to result in loss of sexual desire for women as for men. This overturned the conventional belief that women need intimacy and familiarity in order to engage sexually.  
Marta Meana’s further research found that the problem was that familiarity and intimacy could actually kill women’s sexual desire. For straight men, by contrast, being able to have sex with a long-term partner actually gave them a much greater chance of being sexually satisfied.  
In her recent research on women’s “erotic self-focus,” Meana found that women who had sex in front of a mirror looked at themselves far more often than they looked at their partners.  
Alicia Walker of Missouri State University interviewed 46 female users of the Ashley Madison extramarital-dating website and found that having sex with people outside the relationship was, for them, a way of maintaining their primary relationships. This shattered several myths, including the ideas that women cheat because they are unhappy in their marriages, that women want emotional connection rather than sexual satisfaction, and that women cheat only accidentally.  
Research found that when women were assured that casual sex would be both pleasurable and safe, they were just as likely as men to have it (T. D. Conley, 2011); women chose sexual partners based on sexual attractiveness just as often as men (P. W. Eastwick, 2008); and among people under 40, there was no difference between men and women in rates of non-monogamy (M. W. Wiederman, 1997).  

A married woman, Sarah, fell in love with Paul. After several dates, he invited her to a hotel. She had been intensely aroused during the previous week, but there was always a voice telling her that what she was doing was wrong. In the end, she did not cross that line that day (which she later regretted). She became too nervous, overwhelmed by guilt, and began to cry. Afterwards, Paul either felt hurt and rejected or was simply annoyed, and he cut off contact.  
The double standard applied to men and women when it came to adultery has deep historical roots.  
A 2008 study by the Institute for Economic Studies found that 27% of women fell into poverty after divorce, three times the rate for men. For Sarah, getting divorced meant sacrificing everything, so she unconsciously calculated the costs and benefits in her mind.  
Sarah eventually decided that wanting to be with Paul was a sign that her marriage was beyond saving. In the end, she divorced. She dated several men afterward, but none of it compared with being with Paul.  
Compared with today, women who had affairs in the 1960s were not treated as pathological figures. An affair could serve as a bridge through a transitional period, a potential exit from one marriage into the next.  
They first spent their younger years saying “I do,” with colorful sex within marriage; today we have sexual experience first and settle down afterward. They could never have imagined it.  
Modern Manhattanite Annika, who had immigrated from the Nordic countries, said that America was extremely conservative and that sex education was inadequate. In the Nordic countries, she said, it was practically shown to you firsthand. It was perfectly ordinary for Nordic girls to bring their boyfriends home for the night, whereas in America everything had to be kept secret. Back home in the Nordic region, there were excellent childcare services that supported women after childbirth in all kinds of ways; by comparison, America had nothing.  
Before giving birth, Annika’s husband lost interest in sex, and she lived a colorful life of affairs. After childbirth, her husband persuaded her to move with the whole family to the suburbs. Occupied with raising the children and physically and mentally exhausted, she was no longer able to have affairs.  
She eventually discovered that the real purpose of the move had been to make it easier for him to meet his affair partners while isolating his wife.  
It was like the Malian tradition of forcing women to stay in menstrual huts during their periods.  
Research found that when rhesus monkeys were kept in cages, mating was always initiated by the males. Once they were no longer confined, however, the females became the initiators, persistently following the males and even slapping the ground to demand sex.  
After mating with all the males they lived with for several years, they became bored. But once something new came along, they became sexually frenzied again.  
Human women are the same. It was only with the rise of agriculture that they became subordinate.  

Anthropologists are gradually reaching a consensus that humans did not evolve into monogamous creatures, but instead evolved cooperative child-rearing alongside continual multiple mating that reinforced social bonds.  
Indigenous women in the Americas could exercise sexual autonomy. Among the Huaorani, there was even a ritual in which female desire could help save a life: a girl would identify the boy she wanted to sleep with, and the next day the two would be summoned together before a sick woman to perform a ritual.  
Hoe-based agricultural cultures still required women’s labor. But under plow agriculture, men suddenly gained a physical advantage, leaving women as the sole dependent population. They lost autonomy entirely and became costly possessions maintained by men, while the problem of uncertain paternity became more troublesome than at any other point in history.  
In the Bible, Jezebel, the Phoenician princess who worshipped Baal and caused her husband’s kingdom to convert to her religion, represents women’s power in the pre-plow era. After the rise of plow agriculture, she was repeatedly rewritten, and the metaphor of adultery kept reappearing, with uncontrolled female desire used as a metaphor for worshipping gods other than the one true God.  
Even the famously sexually indulgent ancient Romans were subject to a new moral code under Augustus, who went so far as to exile his promiscuous daughter Julia.  
In 2013, a team of economists from Harvard and UCLA pointed out that the plow was a key factor shaping attitudes toward gender and the degree of female autonomy. Even cultures that had used the plow only once—and whose contemporary environments had changed dramatically—showed lower levels of women’s rights. People were more likely to believe that jobs should be given to men first and that political leaders were better suited to be men.  
Today’s etiquette and gender norms—including women believing they can belong to only one man, being taught from childhood to sit with their legs together, and the ideas that men are naturally highly sexual and entitled to be promiscuous while women should stay at home—are all legacies of the intolerant plow culture.  
The WHO stated in 2012 that the main factors placing women at risk of sexual violence were gender inequality and cultures that demanded sexual purity.  
Large numbers of American women are fascinated by vaginal rejuvenation surgeries whose effectiveness has not been proven, simply in order to please men.  

Among the Himba of Africa, polygyny is practiced but adultery is not taboo. Marriages are sometimes arranged by parents and sometimes freely chosen. Professor Scherz explored how the two arrangements affected the life histories of each woman’s children.  
Since Darwin, sexual-selection theory has emphasized males competing for females, portraying females as passive. In 1856, a 24-year-old married woman who wanted sex was regarded as ill and subjected to abusive “treatments” designed to suppress her sexual desire (Groneman, *Nymphomania: A History*).  
Angus Bateman’s experiments showed that male fruit flies’ reproductive success increased exponentially with the number of mates, whereas female fruit flies could not benefit from multiple mating in the same way. This theory had a major influence on the formation of gender roles at the time.  
The Darwin–Bateman paradigm was challenged by primatologist Sarah Blaffer Hrdy, who discovered that female langurs used promiscuous mating strategies to protect their offspring (male monkeys would kill the offspring of females they had not mated with). She wrote *The Woman That Never Evolved*, and further evidence of female promiscuity began to emerge.  
Female primates did not consciously think through reproductive strategies; they simply felt that it was “comfortable” because of their physiology. Humans have inherited this legacy as well.  
The density of nerve receptors in the clitoris is fourteen times that of the most sensitive part of the penis, and its only function is pleasure. As Rachel Carlton Abrams says in *The Multi-Orgasmic Woman*, women can have multiple orgasms; Hrdy argues that this is what drives female primates to seek repeated mating.  
The female orgasm is elusive. Elizabeth Gordon’s research found that women need ten to twenty minutes of foreplay and stimulation before orgasm, while men need only two and a half to eight minutes.  
When having sex with a familiar partner, heterosexual men reach orgasm 22% more often than women (J. R. Garcia, 2014).  
Hrdy points out that female humans, chimpanzees, bonobos, and macaques all have the same O-shaped orgasmic facial expression, the “O face.”  
*Back to Eden? How Sex in Ancient Times Shaped Who We Are Today* points out that the human cervix, like that of macaques, can filter sperm.  
Men’s testicles are larger than those of gorillas and similar to those of chimpanzees, indicating sperm competition. The coronal ridge is effective at removing another man’s semen; the penis ultimately ejaculates a substance resembling a spermicide that blocks subsequent competitors.  
Primatologist Zanna Clay found that bonobo sexual swelling and mating calls are signals sent by females to males within hearing range.  
Taken together, physiology suggests that women are suited to experimenting sexually all day and all night, which is incompatible with cultural constraints.  
The fewer women there are, the more autonomous they are, the less capable the men are, and the less they inherit, the more often women have more than one partner.  
Anthropologists have found at least eighteen tribes in which children can have more than one father, known as partible paternity. Among the Aché of eastern Paraguay, different kinds of fathers are called by different terms. Stephen Beckerman found that 80% of Aché children with a second father survived to age 15, compared with only 64% of those with just one father.  
Many scholars believe that a cooperative child-rearing culture involving multiple sexual partners was one of the reasons for Homo sapiens’ success.  
On a fair comparison, sperm are not cheap but costly; combined with the lower success rate of fatherless children, lifetime reproductive success was numerically similar for males and females, further undermining the assumption that males were responsible for spreading their seed everywhere.  
Patricia Gowaty found in 2012 that the results of Bateman’s enormously influential experiment could not be replicated.  
Scherz’s research found that nearly 32% of married Himba women had given birth to at least one child fathered by an affair partner during their lives. These were called omoka children—omoka means going far away to fetch water and was a euphemism for a secret rendezvous. Among children of married mothers, 18% were fathered by someone other than their husbands. When Himba women asked Scherz why he slept alone in a tent, he replied that he was married, which they found hilarious.  
Himba men tolerate uncertain paternity because women control inheritance resources, marrying a wife requires no bride price, children contribute to the group from a young age, and they require relatively little care. Affairs also help with childcare and lower the costs involved. The men themselves often have lovers at distant cattle camps.  
Why, then, were only 32% of children born from affairs? Scherz found that all the marriages in which women had affair children had been arranged; none had been freely chosen for love, and none of the freely chosen marriages produced an affair child.  
Why accept an arranged marriage? Compared with freely chosen unions, women who had arranged marriages and lovers had more children who survived beyond age five, demonstrating a reproductive-success advantage to multiple mating.
